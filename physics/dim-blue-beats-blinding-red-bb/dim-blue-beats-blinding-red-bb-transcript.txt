Bear's Notes Why a Dim Blue Lamp Beats a Blinding Red One Aim the brightest red lamp you own at a metal plate — and not one electron escapes. Swap in a dim blue glow, and electrons fly off the instant it lands. Crank the red lamp up to blinding, pour on more and more light — and still, nothing. Brightness is energy, so more ought to mean more — yet here it means nothing at all. A wave washes its energy smoothly over every electron, so enough brightness should eventually shake any one of them loose. But below a certain color, no brightness and no patience ever free a single electron — so the smooth-wave picture is simply wrong here. So stop picturing a smooth wave, and picture light arriving as separate packets. Each packet carries a fixed energy set only by its color — red packets weak, blue packets strong. Turn up the brightness and you get more packets per second, never fatter ones. Every electron sits in the metal behind a fixed escape cost it must be handed all at once to break free. And a packet is swallowed whole by one electron — never shared as sips among many. A weak red packet lands, falls short of the escape cost, and its energy just bleeds away as heat. Pour on millions of red packets and every one still falls short — a flood of near-misses frees nothing. Now one faint blue packet lands, clears the escape cost in a single hit, and an electron flies free. Whatever energy is left after paying the cost becomes that electron's speed. So a packet's energy is just a constant times its frequency — write it E equals h nu — and that packet of light has a name: a photon. An electron escapes only when h nu beats the cost, and the leftover is its kinetic energy: K equals h nu minus the escape cost. Below the frequency where h nu exactly equals the cost, no packet can ever pay — that's the threshold color. Now the mystery dissolves: brightness only counts the packets, but color decides whether any single packet can pay. A blinding red lamp is a torrent of packets that each fall short; a dim blue lamp is a trickle that each clear the bar. On sodium, ultraviolet at three hundred nanometers brings four point one electron volts against a two point three cost, and electrons leave with energy to spare. Slide to green, and each packet lands a hair below the cost — now not one electron leaves, not even under a ten thousand watt lamp. We didn't measure the constant h itself, or ask how one packet can also behave like a wave — each is its own video. Try it yourself: look up a metal's escape cost and find the longest wavelength of light that can still free an electron.
