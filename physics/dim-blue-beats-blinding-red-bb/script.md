**Scope — the ONE insight:** a packet of light carries a fixed energy set by its
*color*, and freeing an electron is a single-packet, all-or-nothing payment against
the metal's escape cost — so the *color* of light, not its *brightness*, decides
whether electrons fly (E = hν, K_max = hν − Φ).
**Classification:** *expository / key-case* — the anomaly (a blinding red lamp frees
nothing; a dim blue lamp frees electrons instantly) is the tension. Brightness, which
classically *is* energy, turns out to be irrelevant.
**Deliberately out of scope (deferred):** Millikan's measurement of h/e and the
stopping potential; photon momentum h/λ; *how* a single packet can also be a wave
(→ concept #3, double slit). Logged, not smuggled in "for completeness."

## Script (one sentence = one beat)

**INTRO**
> Bear's Notes
>
> Why a Dim Blue Lamp Beats a Blinding Red One

**HOOK — the anomaly (unsolved, zero vocabulary)**
1. Aim the brightest red lamp you own at a metal plate — and not one electron escapes.
2. Swap in a dim blue glow, and electrons fly off the instant it lands.
3. Crank the red lamp up to blinding, pour on more and more light — and still, nothing.
4. Brightness *is* energy, so more ought to mean more — yet here it means nothing at all.

**DEEPEN — the wave picture should work, and doesn't**
5. A wave washes its energy smoothly over every electron, so enough brightness should eventually shake any one of them loose.
6. But below a certain color, no brightness and no patience ever free a single electron — so the smooth-wave picture is simply wrong here.

**INSTANCE 1 — light as packets, energy set by color (shown moving)**
7. [CUT] So stop picturing a smooth wave, and picture light arriving as separate packets.
8. Each packet carries a fixed energy set only by its color — red packets weak, blue packets strong.
9. Turn up the brightness and you get *more* packets per second, never fatter ones.

**INSTANCE 2 — the escape cost, all-or-nothing**
10. Every electron sits in the metal behind a fixed escape cost it must be handed all at once to break free.
11. And a packet is swallowed whole by one electron — never shared as sips among many.

**INSTANCE 3 — the collisions (parametrized, morphing)**
12. A weak red packet lands, falls short of the escape cost, and its energy just bleeds away as heat.
13. Pour on millions of red packets and every one still falls short — a flood of near-misses frees nothing.
14. Now one faint blue packet lands, clears the escape cost in a single hit, and an electron flies free.
15. Whatever energy is left after paying the cost becomes that electron's speed.

**TRANSFORM → ABSTRACTION — named only now, as the endpoint**
16. So a packet's energy is just a constant times its frequency — write it E equals h nu — and that packet of light has a name: a photon.
17. An electron escapes only when h nu beats the cost, and the leftover is its kinetic energy: K equals h nu minus the escape cost.
18. Below the frequency where h nu exactly equals the cost, no packet can ever pay — that's the threshold color.

**PAYOFF — the hook resolved (same lamps back on screen)**
19. Now the mystery dissolves: brightness only counts the packets, but color decides whether any single packet can pay.
20. A blinding red lamp is a torrent of packets that each fall short; a dim blue lamp is a trickle that each clear the bar.

**NUMBER — one concrete case**
21. On sodium, ultraviolet at three hundred nanometers brings four-point-one electron-volts against a two-point-three cost, and electrons leave with energy to spare.
22. Slide to green, and each packet lands a hair below the cost — now not one electron leaves, not even under a ten-thousand-watt lamp.

**BOUNDARY + OUTRO**
23. We didn't measure the constant h itself, or ask how one packet can also behave like a wave — each is its own video.
24. Try it yourself: look up a metal's escape cost and find the longest wavelength of light that can still free an electron.

**OUTRO**
> Thanks for watching Why a Dim Blue Lamp Beats a Blinding Red One — more at youtube dot com, slash, at Nik Bear Brown.
