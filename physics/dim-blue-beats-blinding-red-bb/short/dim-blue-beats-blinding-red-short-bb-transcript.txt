Bear's Notes Why a Dim Blue Lamp Beats a Blinding Red One Aim the brightest red lamp you own at a metal plate — and not one electron escapes. Swap in a dim blue glow, and electrons fly off instantly. Crank the red to blinding and still — nothing. So stop picturing a smooth wave: light arrives as separate packets. Each packet's energy is set only by its color — red packets weak, blue packets strong. Brightness just means more packets per second, never fatter ones. And an electron breaks free only if one single packet pays its whole escape cost at once. Every red packet falls short — a flood of near-misses frees nothing. One faint blue packet clears the cost in a single hit, and the electron flies. That's E equals h nu: color is energy, and the packet is called a photon. Brightness counts the packets; color decides whether any one of them can pay. The full story — the leftover energy, the threshold color, the sodium numbers — is in the long video.
