Bear's Notes One Electron Interferes With Itself Fire electrons at two slits one at a time — each arrives as a single dot. Ten in, the dots look random; common sense says two heaps, one behind each slit. Seventy thousand in: stripes — bright and dark bands no two-heap picture allows. Stripes are what waves do: ripples through both slits overlap and cancel. Particles can't do that — a ball takes one slit and joins its heap. And these electrons flew alone: never a second one in the machine to interfere with. So each electron's wave goes through both slits and interferes with itself. The dot is where it's found; the stripes map where finding it is likely — psi squared. Peek at which slit it took, and the stripes vanish — information kills them. Molecules of two thousand atoms paint the same stripes; your wavelength is just too small to show it.
