Bear's Notes One Electron Interferes With Itself Fire electrons at two slits so slowly that only one is ever in the machine — each arrives as a single dot. Ten electrons in, the dots look like random specks — no pattern at all. Common sense says they should pile into two heaps, one behind each slit. Keep firing, and something impossible begins to show. Ripples passing through two openings overlap — crest on crest brightens, crest on trough cancels. So a wave hitting two slits paints stripes on the far screen: strong, weak, strong. A ball can't overlap with anything: it takes one slit or the other. Balls through two slits stack into exactly two heaps — nothing in between, no stripes. Now watch the real electrons: two hundred dots, and a faint clustering appears. Six thousand dots, and unmistakable stripes are forming. Seventy thousand dots: clean bright and dark bands — a full interference pattern. Lay the wave's stripe pattern over the dots, and it fits perfectly. And there are bands where electrons never land — forbidden zones no two-heap story can explain. Remember: the electrons flew alone — there was never a second electron to interfere with. So each electron's wave passes through both slits at once and interferes with itself. The dot is just where the electron is found; the stripes map where finding it is likely. That likelihood map has a name: psi squared, the square of the electron's wave function. And the stripe spacing is set by the electron's wavelength — lambda equals h over p, de Broglie's rule. One electron arrives like a particle and travels like a wave — both, every single time. Peek at which slit it took, and the stripes vanish — not because you jostled it, but because you know. This isn't just electrons: molecules of two thousand atoms have painted the same stripes. You have a wavelength too — walking, about ten to the minus thirty-five meters, far too small for any doorway to reveal. We didn't say what the wave function actually is, or how crystals first revealed matter waves — each is its own video. Try it yourself: compute your own de Broglie wavelength, h over m times v, on your next walk.
