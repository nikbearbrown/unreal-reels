Why One Electron at a Time Still Builds Stripes — Bear's Doodles

Fire electrons at a screen one at a time, so alone that only one is ever in flight. Each should land as a single dot — a particle, with nothing to interfere with.

Set up two narrow slits with a detector screen waiting behind them. The first electrons strike the screen as scattered, random dots. After a hundred, the dots still look like meaningless speckle. But after seventy thousand, the dots stack into sharp bright and dark stripes. Those stripes are an interference pattern — the signature of overlapping waves. Each electron's wave passed through both slits at once and interfered with itself. That self-interference sets the odds of where its single dot can land. Any one dot is random; the crowd of them traces those odds exactly. A particle too small to split still travels as a wave of possibility.
